A Pilot Is Not a Mini Final Project

A pilot is useful if it shows:

  • the workflow can run
  • the output is relevant
  • the assumptions are visible
  • the weak points are now obvious

A pilot that reveals a problem is more valuable than one that confirms a hope.

Case: The 120-Watt Assumption

Every thermal comfort standard assumes the same thing about a seated office worker:

They produce 120 watts of metabolic heat.

That number enters every PMV calculation, every cooling-load estimate, every energy model. It looks precise. It is not.

What Happens When You Replace It

A recent study replaced the uniform 120 W assumption with demographic-aware metabolic rates — accounting for age, sex, and body composition.

Results:

  • simulated HVAC site energy changed by 4–10%
  • the predicted comfort gap between male and female occupants narrowed by 47%

The assumption looked like a fact. It was a design choice — and it had consequences for who gets comfortable.

Why This Matters for Your Pilot

Every workflow has a version of the 120-watt assumption:

  • a default occupant schedule
  • a standard climate file
  • a uniform pedestrian speed
  • a fixed noise threshold

Your pilot should name its assumptions, not hide them inside defaults.

Where Uncertainty Comes From

  • incomplete data — missing readings, sparse surveys
  • noisy observations — sensor drift, subjective responses
  • model assumptions — the 120 W default, fixed schedules
  • small samples — three sites, not thirty
  • pseudo-replication — are 120 readings from one room 120 observations, or one?
  • inconsistent definitions — what counts as “comfortable”?
  • data treatment choices — how you handle gaps changes the answer

Case: Same Data, Different Treatment, Different Conclusion

A study of thermal comfort databases encountered missing radiant-temperature readings — a common problem in field research.

Three treatments of the same missing data:

Treatment Air-to-radiant sensitivity ratio
Rule-based imputation 1.9
Learning through missingness 4.5
Measured-only subset (different again)

The learning model and validation splits were held fixed. Only the treatment of gaps changed. The interpretation of the same evidence shifted by more than 2×.

The Lesson

It is not enough to say “I cleaned the data.”

You must also ask: would a different reasonable cleaning change my conclusion?

If yes, that is not a flaw in your work. It is an uncertainty you should report.

Probability vs. Point Prediction

Traditional thermal comfort gives you a single number:

“PMV = 0.3, therefore comfortable.”

But research on ordinal learning shows that thermal sensation is better represented as a probability distribution:

“73% probability of neutral or slightly warm, 18% probability of warm, 9% probability of hot.”

Why Probabilities Matter

The point prediction says: this is the answer.

The distribution says: here is how confident you should be.

For your pilot:

  • a single score hides disagreement
  • a range or distribution makes the uncertainty visible
  • your audience can then decide whether the confidence is enough for the decision at hand

Input Quality vs. Model Complexity

Architecture students often invest in more complex models while using default inputs.

A study of climate-adapted weather files found:

  • the standard weather file (TMY3) had a cool bias that propagated into 16–31% annual heating overestimates in New York
  • in Los Angeles, the same bias reversed sign — the error went the other direction
  • historical-file biases — not statistical methods — dominated the tested future-scenario errors

The weather file mattered more than the model.

The Equivalent in Your Work

Over-invested Under-examined
complex simulation which climate file?
detailed 3D model which occupant schedule?
multi-criteria scoring which weights?
sophisticated GIS layers which boundary definition?

Before making the model more complex, check whether the inputs deserve the precision.

Case: Equal Setpoints, Unequal Outcomes

A residential cooling study set the same thermostat temperature across all units.

The result: equal cooling setpoints produced unequal modeled thermal outcomes across buildings and age groups.

  • occupants aged 65+ experienced higher predicted core temperatures in all 24 tested cases
  • lowering the setpoint helped them but increased cold-discomfort hours for others
  • predicted sensation and physiological state gave different information

Comparison revealed hidden inequity that the uniform policy concealed.

Case: When a Benefit Comes From the Wrong Source

A learned HVAC supervisor was tested against fixed, PMV-based, and adaptive-band policies across 24 scenarios (six cities, two emissions pathways, present and future weather):

  • it saved 3–7% of site energy in every scenario
  • but it increased worst-zone warm-temperature exposure in every scenario too

Matched ablations showed that the setpoint bounds — not the learning — drove the savings.

The apparent benefit came from the operating constraints, not the intelligence.

Why Ablation Matters

If you compare option A against option B and A wins, ask:

  • which part of A produced the benefit?
  • would a simpler version of A do the same thing?
  • is the benefit real, or is it an artifact of the comparison setup?

This is the difference between “my method works” and “I know why it works.”

Uncertainty Audit: Your Own Pilot

For your current A3 prototype, answer these five questions:

  1. What is the single most influential assumption?
  2. What happens if that assumption is wrong by 20%?
  3. Are your observations independent, or pseudo-replicated?
  4. Would a different reasonable data treatment change the conclusion?
  5. Does your output report a point value or a range?

Write down the answers. They belong in your A3 submission.

Better Language for Results

Instead of:

  • “the data proves”
  • “this shows that”
  • “clearly demonstrates”

Try:

  • “the pilot suggests”
  • “the comparison indicates, subject to [named limit]”
  • “confidence is limited by [specific source]”
  • “under these assumptions, the result favors X”
  • “a different treatment of [gap/input] could change this”

Hedging is not weakness. It is precision.

Comparison Logic

A good comparison requires:

  • stated criteria — before you compare, not after
  • controlled differences — change one thing, hold the rest
  • named assumptions — which defaults are you accepting?
  • sensitivity check — does the ranking survive a reasonable change in inputs?
  • provenance — which data came from measurement, which from a model, which from a standard?

If your recommendation changes when one input shifts by 20%, say so.

Peer Critique Protocol

In pairs, exchange your A3 prototype and answer:

  1. Is the output genuinely tied to the decision?
  2. What is the single most influential assumption?
  3. Is uncertainty being hidden or handled?
  4. Is the pathway still the right one?
  5. What one fix would improve the method most?

Record the critique. Do not defend during the exchange.

A3 Submission Standard

Show enough to demonstrate:

  • one working workflow
  • one interpretable output
  • one clear account of limits

Name the assumptions. Name the uncertainties. Name what you would check next.

Messy is acceptable. Opaque is not.

Week 3: Planning a Test

The question: How do we design a systematic test?

Key concepts: - Proxy measurements vs. preset simulations - Pre-registration mindset: document assumptions BEFORE testing - Clear hypotheses and test protocols - Building performance simulation basics

What you did: - Drafted A3 test plan outline - Began A2 evidence mapping - Explored simulation tools relevant to your project

The Four Assignments

A1: Decision Claim

What it was: - Design object (what are you designing?) - Testable claim (what do you want to know?) - Stakeholder identification (who cares?) - Initial framing of research question

What you learned: - How to frame testable questions - Difference between opinion and testable claim - Stakeholder analysis basics - Research question development

The challenge: Moving from “I think this is good design” to “I claim that X affects Y, and here’s how I’ll test it”

Why it mattered: Everything else built on this foundation. A clear question enables systematic investigation.

A2: Evidence Map

What it was: - Literature review (3-5 pages) - Evidence synthesis from multiple sources - Gap analysis: what’s known vs. what you need to know - Foundation for your investigation

What you learned: - Systematic literature search - Evidence evaluation and synthesis - Identifying knowledge gaps - Building on existing research

The challenge: Finding relevant research when you’re not trained as a researcher, and architectural literature is scattered

Why it mattered: You need to know what’s already known before doing new work. No point re-inventing the wheel.

A3: Test Plan + Pilot

What it was: - Detailed test plan with clear methods - Pre-registered assumptions and hypotheses - Pilot test execution - Pilot memo documenting surprises and adjustments

What you learned: - Method design and protocol documentation - Pre-registration mindset (no p-hacking!) - Reality vs. theory: pilot testing reveals gaps - Adaptive planning based on pilot results

The challenge: Plans look great until you try them. Pilots reveal all the things you didn’t anticipate.

Why it mattered: Better to discover problems in a pilot than after collecting all your data. Systematic testing requires clear protocols.

A4: Final Package

What it is (and what you’re finalizing):

1. Research Brief (8-12 pages): - Complete documentation of investigation - Methods, results, recommendations - Professional research communication

2. Practice Case (2 slides + 2 pages): - Stakeholder-focused summary - Decision thresholds and recommendations - Real-world application format

3. Object Card (1-page poster): - Visual summary of entire project - Portfolio-quality design - Communicates key message at a glance

4. Reproducibility Capsule (3-5 pages): - Step-by-step workflow documentation - Tool requirements and alternatives - Quality control and troubleshooting - Enables others to reproduce or adapt

What you learned: - Professional research documentation - Multi-format communication (different audiences) - Visual design for technical content - Reproducible research practices - Integration across formats

The challenge: Maintaining consistency across four different formats while targeting different audiences

Why it matters: Real-world design research requires multiple communication formats. Clients want memos, peers want briefs, portfolios need visuals.

Key Concepts Across the Semester

Evidence-Based Design Thinking

What it means: Using systematic investigation and data to inform design decisions, while acknowledging uncertainty

Not: Replacing design intuition But: Complementing intuition with evidence

Core principles: 1. Ask testable questions 2. Investigate systematically 3. Acknowledge uncertainty 4. Communicate appropriately 5. Make defendable recommendations

Reproducibility and Transparency

Why it matters: - Others can verify your work - You can revisit and update - Builds trust with stakeholders - Advances the field

How you did it: - Documented all assumptions - Clear method descriptions - Reproducibility Capsule - Change logs tracking revisions

Uncertainty and Confidence

Key insight: All results have uncertainty. Communicating uncertainty builds credibility.

How you learned it: - Sensitivity analysis (Week 4) - Confidence intervals and error bars - Appropriate hedging in language - Threshold setting with ranges

Professional standard: “I’m 80% confident that X is between A and B” is better than “X is definitely C”

Stakeholder-Centered Communication

Key insight: Different audiences need different information

The formats you learned: - Research Brief: For peers and technical audiences - Practice Case: For clients and decision-makers - Object Card: For general audiences and portfolios - Reproducibility Capsule: For researchers and practitioners

Professional skill: Translating between technical and non-technical communication

Systematic Investigation

The process you learned:

  1. Frame the question (A1)
  2. Review existing knowledge (A2)
  3. Design the test (A3 plan)
  4. Pilot and adjust (A3 pilot)
  5. Execute and analyze (A4)
  6. Communicate results (A4 multiple formats)

This is the scientific method, adapted for design

Adaptive Planning

Key insight: Plans change when they meet reality. That’s okay and expected.

How you practiced: - Pilot tests revealed needed adjustments - Peer review highlighted blind spots - Mentor consultations provided guidance - Draft defense showed final priorities

Professional mindset: Rigorous does not mean rigid. Systematic investigation includes systematic adjustment.

Week 11: Final Package Clinic

Your Project Claim, the Abstract, and the Evidence Behind It

Preparation for the evidence clinic

Bring one claim from your own project and the literature you think supports it.

  • a research idea, method choice or studio design decision
  • one relevant paper, its abstract and full text where accessible
  • your current drawing, result or A4 paragraph
  • the question you need the evidence to answer

Open the Evidence Clinic Card. Keep your initial claim visible when you revise it.

A RIS file and OpenAlex key are optional. If access fails, record what remains unverified and use the shared paper to practise the reading method.

Four students: two PhD and two undergraduate students. All undertake the same evidence task on their own projects. There is no assumption that the undergraduate students need a systematic review. The shared case teaches how to inspect evidence; it does not establish support for their individual projects. Keep source and account preparation short so it does not consume clinic time.

Brainstorm: the claim you are relying on

8 minutes · write before discussing

  1. Decision: what do you want to investigate, choose or change?
  2. Claim: what must be true for that choice to make sense?
  3. Evidence: what observation would support or challenge it?
  4. Consequence: what would you change if the evidence did not support it?

“My project proposes . It relies on the claim that . I need evidence about ___ before I can justify ___.”

One task, different project decisions

Your project may ask… The literature must help you establish… Inspect later

Search operators and regex

Tool Example What it does

OpenAlex search "courtyard ventilation" AND housing Combines search terms using the service’s query syntax
Regex on text you hold \bshade\b Finds character patterns in an export, document or filename

Google’s ordinary search box uses search operators. It does not execute a general regex pattern. An exclusion may also hide a useful result; inspect what your query misses.

Google operators · OpenAlex search syntax · examples are teaching queries, not completed searches.

Regex: a pattern in characters

Regular expression (regex): a compact rule for finding or checking text.

Symbol Meaning in these JavaScript examples Example
\b Word boundary \bshade\b matches shade, not shaded
() Group part of a pattern (shade) groups the letters
[0-9]{2} Exactly two digits 01, 12
^ and $ Start/end of the text; of each line with flag m Whole-line filename check
\. A literal full stop .csv extension

Alternatives: (shade|shading) means either word. Flags: i ignores letter case; m makes anchors work per line. Syntax can differ between tools.

MDN regex guide · try the local Regex Lab. Predict one match and one miss before running.

Two patterns, two different checks

Find a design term

\b(shade|shading)\b with i

Matches SHADE and shading. Misses shaded and overhang.

A match in “no shade benefit” still contains the word. Regex has not judged the claim.

Check a filename

^variant_[0-9]{2}\.csv$ with m

Matches variant_03.csv.

Rejects variant_3.csv and variant_03.csv.bak. This checks a naming rule, not the file’s contents.

A false positive is an unwanted match. A false negative is a relevant item missed. Judge both against your purpose.

RIS carries records, not a whole argument

ScienceDirect can export citations as RIS, a tagged text format. Depending on the export, an abstract may be present or absent. Inspect the file you actually have.

DO  - 10.1098/rsos.241337

Field extraction example: ^DO[ \t]+-[ \t]+(.+)$ with m captures the text after the DOI tag. + means one or more; parentheses retain the captured part.

Preserve the raw export. Use a RIS parser for records, repeated authors and wrapped fields. Regex can assist a specific cleanup; it cannot recover methods or results that were never exported.

ScienceDirect export instructions. The DOI line illustrates the format using the shared paper’s identifier; it is not a ScienceDirect export of that paper.

OpenAlex: identify the paper and keep the trail

  1. Look up the DOI and retain the returned work ID.
  2. If needed, search the title and compare authors/year before accepting a candidate.
  3. Record discrepancies and missing abstracts. A match establishes a record link, not support for your project claim.

Optional preparation: create an account, verify your email, then obtain your key from Settings → API key. Keep it private.

The next slides are reading prompts for this block, not additional lecture time. Use the two shared-case slides only as a brief demonstration or fallback. A student who already knows the full paper can record their earlier abstract-based expectation honestly rather than pretend the reading is blind.

First reading: what did you infer?

Read the abstract or exported summary before inspecting the rest of the article.

“The abstract reports . I took this to support in my project. I was about to choose/test/change ___.”

Separate the words in the source from the step you added. Record whether your claim concerns a method, a measured effect or transfer to your own context.

Which part of your project claim is actually stated, and which part is your inference?

Full-paper reading: locate the missing conditions

Inspect Question for your project
Methods What was studied, where, with which sample, inputs and controls?
Results Which measure, comparator and result support the claim?
Figures/tables What variation, exception or failure does the summary omit?
Limitations/discussion Where do the authors restrict interpretation or transfer?
Supplement, if needed Is the required definition, dataset or procedure there?

Save a section and table/figure/page locator. A link alone does not show where your claim gets its support.

Full text provides more evidence to assess. It does not automatically validate a study or make its findings transferable.

Why the abstract may change your impression

Information compressed or absent A possible consequence for your idea
Metric or comparator “Improvement” may refer to a proxy rather than the outcome you need
Sample and conditions A result may support a narrower population, season or setting
Method and validation A simulation, association or exploratory study may need a different claim
Mixed results and limitations The design benefit may depend on a trade-off or unresolved assumption

These are questions to investigate, not accusations against the paper. An abstract may be accurate while leaving out details that matter to your use of it.

Shared paper: the abstract-level view

Mora & Bardhan · Cambridge retrofit study · 2025

The abstract presents a proof-of-concept retrofit-prioritisation framework, combining neighbourhood data with building-scale thermal imaging.

Our project question: could I use thermal imaging to choose which building should be retrofitted first?

What would you need to know before accepting that use of the method?

Read the abstract and full article in HTML · Towards carbon neutrality: mapping mass retrofit opportunities in Cambridge, UK, DOI 10.1098/rsos.241337. The project question is our application, not the authors’ wording.

Shared paper: what the full text adds

§3.1.3: the thermal campaign covers five buildings per cluster, 25 in total.

§4.4: the authors acknowledge limited sampling and unknown thermostat settings and occupancy schedules during data collection.

Our revised use: treat the method as a starting point for investigation. Before relying on building-level rankings in our project, check operating conditions and suitability of the sample.

The details change the justification and the next test. They do not by themselves show that the paper is wrong.

Full-text sections 3.1.3 and 4.4 · this is a teaching interpretation of a published study. Apply the same inspection to your own source.

A defensible before-and-after claim

Before: I used this source to justify ___ in my project.

Found: section/table/figure ___ establishes ___ under . It leaves unresolved.

After: I can now justify , so I will change/retain and test ___ next.

Your conclusion may be supported as stated, needs narrowing/adaptation, contradicted by the inspected evidence, or not yet established. Give a reason; these are course working labels.

Keeping a claim is valid if you show why it survives the check. Do not invent a flaw to complete the exercise.

If an LLM reads only the abstracts

Its answer is constrained by the text you supplied. A fluent synthesis can conceal that the relevant methods, values or limitations were absent.

For every consequential statement, require:

  • a source and exact supporting location
  • the conditions under which it holds
  • a visible not reported / not checked when evidence is missing

Do not let a generated explanation fill missing methods or merge different studies into an unsupported general result. Compare the answer with the actual article before using it to justify a project decision.

RIS is a container format, not a measure of completeness. OpenAlex abstract fields can also be missing. Field documentation.

The clinic ends with an action in your own project. A literature list alone is not the outcome.

Each clinic takes four minutes

Time Lead What happens
0–2 Project author Show the initial claim, source location and revised interpretation
2–4 Peers + instructor Challenge one transfer assumption and agree a feasible next action

What changes in this project because we now know how the source produced its result?

The author records whether to accept, adapt, defer or reject the suggestion, with a reason. Everyone takes a turn as author and contributes to another project.

Twenty minutes to change the work

Choose the action your evidence supports:

  • Research premise: narrow the hypothesis or identify the unresolved comparison.
  • Method: revise a sampling, control, metric or validation choice.
  • Studio: revise a design option or draw the contextual condition that needs checking.
  • Claim: correct the A4 sentence and show the design consequence.

Before a test, write the expected observation and what result would change your decision. Save the actual result if attempted.

If access, time or tools block the check, save the exact gap and next action. A planned test stays labelled as planned.

Pair recheck: can someone follow the evidence?

Eight minutes · four per project

Your partner opens the cited section or figure and checks:

  1. Is the source finding represented accurately?
  2. Are its conditions and your transfer assumption visible?
  3. Does the revised project decision follow?
  4. What still needs evidence or a local test?

“I can trace ___ to . I still cannot establish because ___.”

Update the record if the recheck exposes another gap.

Six components in the A4 package

Component Today’s evidence check can improve…
Practice Case · 2 slides Decision, recommendation and its scope
Research Brief · 2 pages Source use, method rationale and interpretation
Object Card · 1 page Claim, figure and visible caveat
Reproducibility Capsule Source locations, runnable workflow and verification notes
Feedback + Revision Ledger · ≥3 entries Advice, response, change and reason
Reflection Note · 1 page What changed in your reasoning and what remains unresolved

A4 brief · final checklist. Today’s clinic is formative and changes no assessment requirements or deadlines.

The capsule supports the claim and the rerun

Include the actual runnable artifact, inputs, tool versions, instructions and expected key output. Supporting screenshots explain a workflow; they do not replace its runnable file.

For literature used in the argument, retain the citation, source location, what you checked and any relevant search or transformation notes. Keep API keys out of the package.

Can a peer follow the evidence trail and rerun the workflow that produced your key figure? Record the successful check or exact blocker.

If you explore RIS/OpenAlex further, save raw and processed records separately. Metadata matching alone does not complete a literature review.

The Object Card after the evidence check

Show the revised claim with the figure or drawing it actually supports.

Check What the reader needs
Decision What should change, and for whom?
Evidence Which comparison supports that action?
Conditions Where and when does the statement apply?
Limit What could change the recommendation?

Give the card to a partner for 30 seconds. Ask them to restate the recommendation and one limit without your explanation.

A4 record: reasoning that changed

Final six minutes · save the card and a dated ledger entry

Did What claim and source did I inspect?

Assumed What did I infer from the abstract?

Risk What did the full paper leave uncertain for my project?

Verify What changed or survived, and what test remains?

Retain the original statement, source locators, revised artifact and peer response. Today’s entry contributes to A4’s existing minimum of three ledger entries across the project.

Preparation for the Object Fair

A reader should be able to understand the decision, inspect its evidence and locate the workflow.

  • make the six required components easy to find
  • test the files and the key figure’s rerun
  • prepare an answer about method, limits and alternatives
  • distinguish what you checked from what you would investigate next

Show the project as it now stands, including the uncertainty that still matters to the decision.

Exit: what does the evidence let you claim?

“I initially thought . Reading showed . In my project I will now , because . The next check is .”

If your claim survives, explain why. If support remains unverified, keep that limit visible.

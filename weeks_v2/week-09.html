Week 9: Thresholds, Drawn

Where you draw the line decides the story. Can a reader see it?

Before class (the flip)

Bring · 20 minutes at home

  1. One A3 output: a table, chart, map, or model result.
  2. Two diagram briefs from your own project, written for someone else to draw. Use the brief format on the next slide; there are worked examples for both projects later in the deck.
  3. Spend five minutes with the Threshold Explorer. Move one slider until the recommendation changes.

In class, we do not lecture at you

  • Session 1: we look at drawn threshold diagrams and argue about where the lines are.
  • Session 2: you draw each other’s briefs three ways (hand, AI, data tool) and compare.
  • Three of us: two students and the instructor. The instructor writes two briefs too and is drawn, judged, and challenged like everyone else.

The brief you bring (×2)

Write it so a classmate who does not know your project could draw it.

Field What to write
Decision the choice this diagram supports, in one line

Two example briefs, written for the other person

Pavilion siting (research track)

  • Decision: which Pareto site goes to the estates office
  • Data: 14 sites × (walk min, peak UTCI drop °C) from the simulation run, plus the weather file and analysis hour
  • Threshold: UTCI drop ≥ 4 °C (my guess, or a category drop from the UTCI scale); walk ≤ 4 min (client)
  • Audience: estates office, 30 seconds
  • Must show: the front, the two lines, and which sites survive both

Studio massing (studio track)

  • Decision: which massing to develop for the mid-review
  • Data: 3 options × floors, GFA, and pass/fail on the 25° check
  • Threshold: GFA ≥ 4,000 m² (brief); 25° line from the neighbour (borrowed rule of thumb)
  • Audience: studio tutor, 3-minute crit
  • Must show: the section with the line, and the one option that fits under it

Same exercise, two depths

Undergraduate · studio track

  • one threshold per diagram, drawn on the drawing (section, plan, or elevation)
  • name where the line came from: brief, code, precedent, or guess
  • answer the flip question in words: “If the GFA target rises 5%, C fails.”
  • hand sketch plus one data version is enough

PhD · research track

  • decide which goals are objectives and which are constraints (ε), and justify the split
  • report how the surviving Pareto set changes as each ε moves: a flip chart over ε
  • test the robustness of the result across weather years, analysis hours, or sun-angle sampling
  • defend the threshold definition (a continuous drop vs a category crossing) from the literature, and say which line is your contribution and which you borrowed

In the swap, each of you draws the other track’s brief. Studio eyes on a Pareto plot, research eyes on a section.

Prompt starter for round 2

Paste into any AI tool, then paste your brief “You are drawing a decision diagram for an architecture audience. Draw one figure in which the threshold is a visible line or band inside the plot, the options are labelled directly (no legend), and the title states the takeaway. Use only the numbers in my brief. If you need a number that is not there, ask me. If you can, give me Python/matplotlib code instead of an image.”

Image generators draw pictures of charts They can invent ticks, nudge the line, or round 48.4 down to “under 48”. Fine for a layout sketch, but never as evidence.

Code is checkable A chart made by code you ran is a Week 8 “workflow/code” output: run it, check the units, and test it with one known value.

Defend one line (5 min each)

Each author takes one threshold from their winning diagram. The other two each take one role and attack it once. Rotate roles each round so everyone plays all three.

Roles

  • Client: “That line costs me money.”
  • Occupant: “That line leaves me out.”
  • Regulator: “Where did that number come from?”

Author answers with three things

  1. the source: code, precedent, client, physics, or my guess
  2. how far it can move before the recommendation flips
  3. what evidence would make you move it

The sentence that goes on the card

Recommendation sentence, Week 9 version For [audience], I recommend [option] because it clears [threshold] ([source]). This changes if [threshold] moves by [how much] or if [counter-argument] holds.

Reconstruct your threshold diagram in four steps

Object this week: the diagram of your own brief that you chose, and the line inside it.

Did Which version did you choose (hand, AI, or data), and what is its one message?

Assumed Where does the line come from, and who chose it?

Risk How far can it move before the recommendation flips? What did AI get wrong?

Verify Which number in the figure did you check against the brief?

Post the four steps under your chosen diagram in Slack. The graded unit is the posting plus an honest reconstruction, not how well you spoke.

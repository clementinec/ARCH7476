If you can’t rerun it, it isn’t evidence

The reproducibility frame

A result becomes evidence only when someone else can inspect how you got it — and ideally reproduce it.

The week’s question If you left the project tomorrow, could someone trust your output without you in the room to explain it?

Documentation is not paperwork after the fact. It is the part of the method that makes the output legible.

Where reproducibility quietly fails

These are ordinary, not dramatic — and they happen to careful people.

Failure What was lost
Plugin / version not recorded the file won’t reopen or rerun later
Settings changed but not noted results can’t be matched to inputs
One person held the knowledge nobody else can defend the method
CRS / units left implicit spatial output is silently wrong
AI output treated as final evidence a claim no one actually checked

The risk is not embarrassment. It is presenting a result you can no longer stand behind.

When the spreadsheet silently rewrote the data

Anecdote · the tool changed your data and didn’t tell you

What happened Geneticists found that Excel was auto-converting gene names into dates and numbers (for example “SEPT2” became a date). A 2016 study found errors in roughly one in five papers with supplementary spreadsheets — the field eventually renamed the genes rather than fight the software.

The lesson Reproducibility starts with knowing what your tool did to your data. Document formats, versions, and import settings — the failure is invisible until someone re-runs it.

What happens after you press Send?

Model literacy · 1 of 7

A common baseline is an autoregressive, decoder-only Transformer: it generates a continuation from the context already available.

01 · Input

Tokens

Text is split into units and represented as vectors.

02 · Context

Causal attention

Layers use the available context, not future answer tokens.

03 · Predict

Next token

The model scores possible next tokens; decoding selects one.

04 · Continue

Append and repeat

Append the token to the context; repeat.

Why it matters: the model generates a continuation conditioned on your input. A worked example and a clear format help define the artifact you want.

Do this: specify “inputs in metres → three overhang options → one comparison table.” Check any performance claim against a source or calculation.

Documented decoder example: Qwen2.5 technical report, §2 (2024).

Allow about two minutes, including the two practice-note reveals. Distinguish the neural-network architecture from architecture as a design discipline. Decoder-only means the text backbone does not need a separate encoder–decoder pair; it does not mean a multimodal product has no image or audio encoders. During training, masked token positions can be processed in parallel. This diagram describes the conceptual generation loop, not every serving optimisation. The model learns conditional patterns and representations, not an arithmetic average of answers. An explicit format makes a draft easier to inspect; it does not guarantee correct content. This mechanism can support complex reasoning, but a fluent facade explanation does not establish that a solar calculation was performed.

What can attention connect — and what is missing?

Model literacy · 2 of 7

Illustrative prompt “Compare 0.6 m and 1.2 m overhangs on this west-facing facade. Explain the likely shading trade-off.”

Available context The model can connect the dimensions, orientation, attached material and earlier messages when forming a response.

Still needs evidence An image may omit scale, location, glazing, weather or occupancy. Attention does not supply missing project measurements or run a simulation by itself.

Why it matters: the project context you supply shapes the comparison. Missing site information may become an unstated assumption.

Do this: attach a labelled plan, north arrow, scale, location and relevant brief. Ask it to distinguish supplied facts, assumptions and missing inputs.

Architectural application of the preceding mechanism, not a model benchmark. A model may infer or recall information, but inferred project inputs must be identified and checked. Distinguish the model’s proposed explanation from a tool result. Avoid teaching that next-token prediction prevents reasoning, or that attention guarantees faithful use of every supplied fact.

How does a predictor become an assistant?

Model literacy · 3 of 7

01 · Pre-training

Learn from data

Next-token training develops language, knowledge and patterns useful for many tasks.

02 · Supervised fine-tuning

Learn from examples

Train on demonstrations of useful responses and task behaviour.

03 · Classic RLHF

Learn from preferences

People rank responses → a reward model learns those preferences → reinforcement learning updates the assistant.

RLHF = reinforcement learning from human feedback.

Why it matters: assistant training helps it follow requests and respond usefully. A helpful-sounding endorsement is not an independent design review.

Do this: specify the audience and criteria: client explanation, methods critique or component specification. Ask for the strongest objection, then check its evidence.

One influential recipe, not every model’s training sequence: Ouyang et al., InstructGPT (2022).

About two minutes, including the two practice-note reveals. Training changes model weights; supplying a prompt usually changes the context for that interaction, not the model’s weights. RLHF happens during training, not whenever a person presses a thumbs-up button. Feedback may inform later training, depending on the service. Keep the simplified diagram clearly labelled as the classic recipe. Its reward is a proxy for the qualities being judged, not a certificate of architectural correctness. Do not imply that RLHF inevitably causes agreement or that asking for a critique removes bias.

A preferred answer and a checked answer differ

Model literacy · 4 of 7

Training signal What it can reward Architectural question it leaves open
Human or AI preferences Useful, clear, appropriate responses Does this claim hold for this site and brief?
DPO · direct preference optimisation Preferred responses directly, without classic RLHF’s separate reward-model/RL loop Do the preferences capture the right criteria?
RL with verifiable rewards Checkable outcomes, such as correct answers or passing code tests Are those tests sufficient for the design claim?

Why it matters: passing a test only supports what that test measures. Correct arithmetic for our assumed 3.0 m setback does not establish planning compliance.

Do this: define success first: known cases for code, source fidelity for a literature summary, and explicit comfort/access trade-offs for a design recommendation.

DPO (2023) · Constitutional AI: AI feedback (2022) · DeepSeek-R1: reasoning rewards (2025). The setback is a classroom assumption.

About two minutes. Reward source and optimisation method are different dimensions: DPO may use human or AI preference data. In R1, verifiable math/code rewards are part of a broader training recipe; R1-Zero is the experiment without initial supervised fine-tuning, not a description of every R1 stage. Passing tests provides evidence only for the tested behaviour. Ask what “good” means when energy, access, comfort and design intent conflict.

Stronger systems also do more while answering

Model literacy · 5 of 7

Reasoning effort A model can spend more computation on intermediate steps before answering. Qwen3 documents adjustable thinking budgets; improvement depends on the task.

Tools and retrieved evidence A system can retrieve a document, execute code or call a tool, then use the result. Those actions add evidence only if the inputs and returned results are checked.

propose a step

execute a tool

inspect its result

revise the decision

Why it matters: more reasoning, more evidence and an executed calculation solve different problems. Extra reasoning time cannot itself supply a missing measurement.

Do this: use reasoning for a difficult comparison, retrieval for a source and code for a calculation. Save the actual sources, inputs and tool outputs.

Qwen3 (2025), §§4.3–4.7 · Anthropic: limits of reported reasoning (2025).

About two minutes. Reasoning effort is inference-time computation, not proof of a new backbone architecture. Retrieval supplies context; it is not retraining. A tool invocation is usually orchestrated by software around the model. Request sources, calculations and a concise rationale, rather than treating a generated reasoning narrative as privileged access to internal computation. Anthropic’s study found omissions in reported reasoning under specific experimental conditions; do not generalise its percentages to every model or architectural task.

Is decoder-only still the whole story?

Model literacy · 6 of 7 · evidence checked 4 November 2026

Documented example What it shows What to take into practice
Qwen3 · 2025 Dense and mixture-of-experts (MoE) models; MoE routes each token through selected learned components More parameters need not mean all are used on every token
Nemotron 3 Ultra · June 2026 A hybrid of Mamba state-space layers and attention, with MoE Current designs also change how sequences are processed efficiently
Qwen3.5-Omni · April 2026 Text, vision and audio in a multimodal system A plan or walkthrough can be input; perceived dimensions and performance still need checks

Why it matters: model design changes capability and efficiency, but a bigger parameter count or an “expert” label does not establish skill on your task.

Do this: judge it on a known task from your work: plan reading, document synthesis or tool execution. Record the visible model/mode, result, date and time taken.

Primary reports: Qwen3 · NVIDIA Nemotron 3 Ultra · Qwen3.5-Omni. Examples of mechanisms, not a model ranking.

About two minutes, including the two practice-note reveals. Mamba is a state-space sequence model that maintains and updates a state; hybrids combine it with attention. MoE “experts” are learned network components, not named professionals or necessarily human-readable specialties. Autoregressive generation can survive changes to the internal backbone. Decoder-only remains a useful baseline, not a universal description of every component. These reports document particular public designs; do not infer the undisclosed architecture of a current proprietary service from its output style, latency or benchmark score. One known task is a useful local check, not a general benchmark; log cost if visible, without requiring purchase of a second model.

What might a more capable design assistant do?

Model literacy · 7 of 7 · discussion

Plausible direction · not an established capability claim Read a brief and model → propose facade variants → call geometry and simulation tools → compare results against agreed criteria → return a traceable recommendation.

Potentially more automated File preparation, parameter changes, tool execution, error repair and comparison tables.

Still needs a defensible decision Who defines comfort, acceptable trade-offs and missing evidence? Who checks that the simulation represents the intended design?

Why it matters: automation can repeat a workflow faster. If its criteria miss the design question, faster iteration can optimise the wrong thing.

Do this: delegate one repeatable step. State what may change, what stays fixed, how improvement is judged and when the result needs your review.

Use the final two minutes of the literacy block, including the practice-note reveals. This is a teaching speculation grounded in documented tool use and multimodality, not a promise that a product can reliably complete this whole architectural loop. Invite one response: “Which step would you delegate next, and what check would you keep?” Keep model structure, training and runtime orchestration distinct.

Not every AI output needs the same check

Three output types

AI output type What it is good for Final-gate check
Fact-based reiteration summarize standards, definitions, precedent claims, code-like information verify against primary source, datasheet, standard, or cited document
Relationship hypothesis / interpretation propose explanations for comfort, perception or spatial experience compare with physical mechanisms, empirical evidence, an appropriate simulation or a bounded calculation
Workflow/code drafting produce formulas, scripts, GhPython snippets, notebook cells, checklists run it, inspect units/ranges, test known cases, and log failures

Final gate AI can help generate an answer. You decide which gate it must pass before it enters the design argument.

Human experience is messy enough that AI can help

Why architecture needs this

Architecture often asks about relationships that are real but hard to reduce cleanly:

  • spatial atmosphere and perceived calm
  • thermal acceptability rather than just air temperature
  • acoustic comfort rather than only decibels
  • wayfinding confidence rather than only shortest path
  • facade perception rather than only performance values

A productive use AI can help generate hypotheses for these fuzzy relationships. It cannot validate them. Validation still comes from source checks, simple calculations, field observations, precedent mechanisms, simulations, or sensitivity tests.

AI Fact-Checking Cases

Read each case and name the output type, then decide the final-gate check.

Case Output type Final gate
AI summarizes a daylight standard fact-based reiteration find the standard and quote/check the clause
AI proposes why a shaded courtyard may feel cooler relationship hypothesis / interpretation compare with radiation/wind/shade logic and one bounded metric
AI writes a Python scoring function workflow/code drafting run known cases and inspect units/ranges
AI writes a literature-review paragraph fact + writing support verify every claim and citation before use

The line is rarely “AI or not.” It is what kind of output is this, and what would make it trustworthy enough to use?

Large-Model Method Review

A common situation, examined as a method — not a person.

The artifact A building-performance analysis: polished charts, statistics beyond the author’s stated skill, no understanding of the method when questioned, no disclosure of AI use.

Review it together

  • which specific claims can the author actually defend?
  • which claims are factual, which are relationship-based, and which are workflow/code?
  • what final-gate checks would have made this trustworthy?
  • what should have been disclosed, and where?
  • what is the minimum honest version of this output?

We critique the workflow and its claims, never the author’s ability.

Run one check that could change your mind

Lab 23–38 min · individual, with a peer available · one bounded check

  1. Before checking: record the expected result and its basis — a source, known case, manual calculation or observation.
  2. Perform the check: inspect the source, rerun a saved component, calculate a value or compare independent evidence.
  3. Record what happened: expected versus observed result; pass, fail or pending; what the check cannot establish.

Asking the same model “are you sure?” is not an independent check. A second answer can suggest a test; agreement between answers does not validate the claim.

A peer rerun checks whether instructions and saved files reproduce a result; it does not establish physical validity by itself. For a performance claim, distinguish rerunning code from validating assumptions. Do not require students to complete a substantial simulation in 15 minutes: a bounded known case or an honest pending record is acceptable.

Make the architectural consequence visible

Lab 38–45 min · revise the artifact/claim and complete the existing verification card

Illustrative revision Before: “The AI says this overhang will make the room comfortable.”

After: “The checked shadow geometry supports shade at this time and location. It does not establish thermal comfort. I will retain this option for a temperature, radiation and ventilation comparison.”

  1. Mark the output accept, revise, reject or pending, with a reason.
  2. Save the input, output, check result and revised artifact. Record the visible model/tool name, mode and date; mark unknown details honestly.
  3. Add a dated note to your project page or local draft: peer feedback → check → changed decision → remaining limit.

The sample revision assumes that a shadow-geometry check actually took place. Students must not copy its result without doing an equivalent check. Reference · Documentation Clinic

Document one workflow so it stands alone

The clinic

Pick the most complex or critical workflow in your project — the one you would least want to lose.

Optional extension · 10 minutes Write step-by-step instructions clear enough that a classmate who has never seen your method could follow them and get your result — with no verbal explanation from you.

Candidates: a Grasshopper setup, a survey-data analysis, a GIS operation, a Python notebook, a statistical step.

The documentation swap

We test documentation the only honest way: someone else follows it cold.

Exchange · 2 min Swap with someone whose method differs from yours. No verbal notes — the instructions must stand alone.

Attempt · 8 min Follow their steps exactly. Mark where you get confused, stuck, or have to guess.

Feedback · 5 min Report what was unclear, what was missing, and the one change that would help most.

The gaps you find are the gaps a future reader would hit. This is the most useful feedback you will get all week.

Reference · Mentor Preparation

Make the mentor meeting count

Mentor prep

Most mentor time is lost to vague questions. A little preparation turns a pleasant chat into a useful one.

When a consultation is relevant · 15 minutes

  1. One-liner — your project in a single sentence
  2. Specific ask — the exact advice you need
  3. Context check — what your mentor actually does
  4. Question bank — five strategic questions
  5. Show-and-tell — the one thing you want them to see

Weak questions vs strategic questions

Weak — wastes the slot

  • “What do you think of my project?”
  • “Do you have any advice?”
  • “How do I get a job in your field?”

Strategic — creates value

  • “What evidence would convince [specific stakeholder] to change current practice?”
  • “What implementation barriers am I likely missing?”
  • “How do practitioners here validate this kind of analysis?”
  • “What would make my findings most useful to someone in your position?”

Question workshop

Optional pair exercise · 10 minutes

  1. Person A delivers their one-liner + best strategic question
  2. Person B gives specific feedback and one improvement
  3. Swap roles and repeat

You are not competing for the best question. You are pressure-testing your own so it earns a real answer in the room.

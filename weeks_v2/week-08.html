The reproducibility frame

If you can’t rerun it, it isn’t evidence

A result becomes evidence only when someone else can inspect how you got it — and ideally reproduce it.

The week’s question If you left the project tomorrow, could someone trust your output without you in the room to explain it?

Documentation is not paperwork after the fact. It is the part of the method that makes the output legible.

Where reproducibility quietly fails

These are ordinary, not dramatic — and they happen to careful people.

Failure What was lost
Plugin / version not recorded the file won’t reopen or rerun later
Settings changed but not noted results can’t be matched to inputs
One person held the knowledge nobody else can defend the method
CRS / units left implicit spatial output is silently wrong
AI output treated as final evidence a claim no one actually checked

The risk is not embarrassment. It is presenting a result you can no longer stand behind.

Anecdote · the tool changed your data and didn’t tell you

When the spreadsheet silently rewrote the data

What happened Geneticists found that Excel was auto-converting gene names into dates and numbers (for example “SEPT2” became a date). A 2016 study found errors in roughly one in five papers with supplementary spreadsheets — the field eventually renamed the genes rather than fight the software.

The lesson Reproducibility starts with knowing what your tool did to your data. Document formats, versions, and import settings — the failure is invisible until someone re-runs it.

A quick, honest poll

Have you ever struggled to reconstruct what you did on a project from a few months ago?

Almost everyone has. The point is not guilt — it is to treat documentation as a normal, expected part of the workflow, starting with one process today.

Session 1 · Documentation Clinic

The clinic

Document one workflow so it stands alone

Pick the most complex or critical workflow in your project — the one you would least want to lose.

Do this now · 10 minutes Write step-by-step instructions clear enough that a classmate who has never seen your method could follow them and get your result — with no verbal explanation from you.

Candidates: a Grasshopper setup, a survey-data analysis, a GIS operation, a Python notebook, a statistical step.

Documentation template

Copy this structure into your notes.

Section What to fill in
Workflow the one process you are documenting
Setup software + versions, files/data, prerequisite skills
Steps each step, in order, with the reason for it
Decisions where a choice was made, and why
Troubleshooting common error → what to do
Quality check how to know the result is correct

The documentation swap

We test documentation the only honest way: someone else follows it cold.

Exchange · 2 min Swap with someone whose method differs from yours. No verbal notes — the instructions must stand alone.

Attempt · 8 min Follow their steps exactly. Mark where you get confused, stuck, or have to guess.

Feedback · 5 min Report what was unclear, what was missing, and the one change that would help most.

The gaps you find are the gaps a future reader would hit. This is the most useful feedback you will get all week.

AI as companion

What large models are, for this course

The short version Large models are probabilistic models of patterns in human-produced data. They can be useful companions, but they are not a final gate for truth.

What they tend to do

  • produce plausible common answers
  • average across many writing patterns
  • compress complicated claims into fluent language
  • sound confident even when the source is weak

Why they are still useful

  • they can expose hidden assumptions
  • they can draft small workflow components
  • they can search the shape of an ill-defined relation
  • they can help when human experience is hard to model directly

The skill is not “believe or reject AI.” The skill is using it, then deciding what kind of check must happen before the output becomes evidence.

Large-model literacy

The useful part is also the dangerous part

Common answer problem Because LLMs learn from pooled writing, they often produce the most available or average explanation. That can be helpful for orientation, but it can miss local constraints, updated facts, minority cases, and project-specific physics.

Design implication Do not ask only, “What does AI say?” Ask: what local fact, mechanism, calculation, or observed experience would push this answer away from the common mean?

Three output types

Not every AI output needs the same check

AI output type What it is good for Final-gate check
Fact-based reiteration summarize standards, definitions, precedent claims, code-like information verify against primary source, datasheet, standard, or cited document
Relationship-based simulation reason about fuzzy links: comfort, perception, spatial experience, thermal/acoustic sensation compare with engineering/science logic, empirical evidence, sensitivity test, or bounded calculation
Workflow/code drafting produce formulas, scripts, GhPython snippets, notebook cells, checklists run it, inspect units/ranges, test known cases, and log failures

Final gate AI can help generate an answer. You decide which gate it must pass before it enters the design argument.

Why architecture needs this

Human experience is messy enough that AI can help

Architecture often asks about relationships that are real but hard to reduce cleanly:

  • spatial atmosphere and perceived calm
  • thermal acceptability rather than just air temperature
  • acoustic comfort rather than only decibels
  • wayfinding confidence rather than only shortest path
  • facade perception rather than only performance values

A productive use AI can help generate hypotheses for these fuzzy relationships. It cannot validate them. Validation still comes from source checks, simple calculations, field observations, precedent mechanisms, simulations, or sensitivity tests.

AI Fact-Checking Cases

Read each case and name the output type, then decide the final-gate check.

Case Output type Final gate
AI summarizes a daylight standard fact-based reiteration find the standard and quote/check the clause
AI proposes why a shaded courtyard may feel cooler relationship-based simulation compare with radiation/wind/shade logic and one bounded metric
AI writes a Python scoring function workflow/code drafting run known cases and inspect units/ranges
AI writes a literature-review paragraph fact + writing support verify every claim and citation before use

The line is rarely “AI or not.” It is what kind of output is this, and what would make it trustworthy enough to use?

Large-Model Method Review

A common situation, examined as a method — not a person.

The artifact A building-performance analysis: polished charts, statistics beyond the author’s stated skill, no understanding of the method when questioned, no disclosure of AI use.

Review it together

  • which specific claims can the author actually defend?
  • which claims are factual, which are relationship-based, and which are workflow/code?
  • what final-gate checks would have made this trustworthy?
  • what should have been disclosed, and where?
  • what is the minimum honest version of this output?

We critique the workflow and its claims, never the author’s ability.

Session 1 · Mentor Prep

Mentor prep

Make the mentor meeting count

Most mentor time is lost to vague questions. A little preparation turns a pleasant chat into a useful one.

Prepare now · 15 minutes

  1. One-liner — your project in a single sentence
  2. Specific ask — the exact advice you need
  3. Context check — what your mentor actually does
  4. Question bank — five strategic questions
  5. Show-and-tell — the one thing you want them to see

Weak questions vs strategic questions

Weak — wastes the slot

  • “What do you think of my project?”
  • “Do you have any advice?”
  • “How do I get a job in your field?”

Strategic — creates value

  • “What evidence would convince [specific stakeholder] to change current practice?”
  • “What implementation barriers am I likely missing?”
  • “How do practitioners here validate this kind of analysis?”
  • “What would make my findings most useful to someone in your position?”

Question workshop

In pairs · 10 minutes

  1. Person A delivers their one-liner + best strategic question
  2. Person B gives specific feedback and one improvement
  3. Swap roles and repeat

You are not competing for the best question. You are pressure-testing your own so it earns a real answer in the room.

Follow-up email template

Send within 48 hours. Show that their advice changed something.

Part What to write
Subject “Thank you for your insights on [topic] — [Your name]”
Opening the specific point that shifted your thinking
Action taken the concrete change or next step you made
Progress one short paragraph on how it shaped your work
Next step what you’ll do next; offer to share results

Draft it now Write the subject line and opening for your upcoming mentor meeting before you leave class.

Peer Reproducibility Check

In small groups, run each other’s documentation cold — the same test as the swap, on real project material.

Hand over Share your workflow package: steps, tool + version list, troubleshooting notes, quality check.

Attempt A peer follows it without you explaining. They note exactly where it breaks or needs a guess.

Improve Together, name the single change that would most improve reproducibility.

We are not ranking packages. We are finding, kindly and early, the gap a stranger would fall into.

A2 Worked Example: External Shading Devices for Offices in Hot Climates

When the device that saves the most energy cuts too much daylight

NoteAbout This Example

This is a worked A2 skeleton based on a real research project — “Impact of external shading devices on thermal and daylighting performance of offices in hot climate regions” (Freewan, Solar Energy, 2014). DOI: 10.1016/j.solener.2014.01.009

It starts from a spatial design decision — which shading device to fix to a south-facing office facade — and uses both field measurement and simulation to compare three options. See also the companion data-analysis example for a non-spatial version of the same A2 structure.

1. Decision Recap

Decision

Which fixed external shading device for south-facing offices in a hot-arid climate (Amman, Jordan) best balances cooling-load reduction with maintained task-level daylight?

Alternatives

Option Description
A — Vertical fins External vertical aluminium fins projecting from the facade
B — Diagonal fins External fins mounted at an oblique angle to the facade plane
C — Egg-crate device Combined horizontal-and-vertical grid (egg-crate geometry) projecting from the facade

All three options were tested on identical south-facing offices at the Jordan University of Science and Technology (JUST) campus. Only the shading device changed; the glazing, room dimensions, orientation, and occupancy were held constant. A fourth condition — the unshaded base case — served as the control.

Audience

Architects and building-services engineers selecting a fixed shading system for south-facing office facades in hot-arid climates, where cooling dominates the energy budget but daylight must remain sufficient for desk work.

Why evidence matters

The instinct choice is often the simplest device — vertical fins are familiar, easy to fabricate, and widely used. But Freewan’s study showed that vertical fins allowed large sun patches to penetrate the office in the afternoon, providing the least cooling relief of the three options (~7% cooling-load reduction vs. base case). The egg-crate device delivered the highest cooling savings (~17%) but cut desk-level illuminance to roughly 200 lux — below the 300 lux task threshold. The ranking of the three options reverses depending on whether you lead with energy or daylight. That tension cannot be resolved by instinct; it requires a controlled comparison.

What action the evidence should support

Select one fixed shading device for the facade, with documented evidence showing the trade-off between cooling-load reduction and maintained daylight for each option.

Route Selection

I am choosing Grasshopper + Ladybug/Honeybee (or alternatively a BIM/BEM tool like IES-VE or DesignBuilder, as Freewan used) because my decision depends on comparing cooling loads and daylight levels for three shading devices on the same facade geometry, and the minimum useful output is the cooling-load reduction percentage and desk-level illuminance for each option.

Risk Check

  • Most fragile input: Optical properties of the diagonal fins at oblique angles — manufacturer data gives properties at normal incidence, but shading performance depends on solar angle throughout the day
  • Likely setup problem: Matching the Radiance material definition to the physical aluminium surface; ensuring the simulation grid captures the desk plane accurately
  • What I can cut: Drop the annual sDA/ASE calculation and report point-in-time illuminance at peak cooling hours — this reduces simulation time significantly
  • What I need help with: Validating that the simulation model reproduces the field-measured illuminance values before trusting the annual extrapolation

5. A4 Branch Declaration

Route

Route A: Retrospective Redesign — this example dissects a published study to show how the A2 structure maps onto a real facade decision. A student using Route B would apply the same comparison structure to their own studio project.

Decision

  • Project: South-facing office building, JUST campus, Amman, Jordan
  • Decision: Which fixed external shading device to specify for the south facade
  • Alternatives: Vertical fins (A), diagonal fins (B), egg-crate device (C)
  • Why this matters: The device that saves the most cooling energy (egg-crate, ~17%) drops illuminance below the task threshold (~200 lux), while the device that preserves the most daylight (vertical fins, ~350 lux) saves the least energy (~7%). The ranking reverses depending on which metric leads.

Workflow

  • Primary route: Coupled thermal-daylight simulation (IES-VE, DesignBuilder, or Grasshopper + Honeybee)
  • Input files: Office geometry, EPW, shading device definitions
  • What varies: Shading device type (single controlled variable)
  • Minimum scenarios: 3 shading options + 1 base case (no shading)
  • Output: Cooling-load reduction (%), indoor air temperature reduction, desk-level illuminance per option
  • Threshold: Cooling reduction ≥ 10%; illuminance ≥ 300 lux

Feasibility

  • What must exist by A3: Working simulation model producing cooling load and illuminance for at least Options A and C (the two geometric extremes)
  • Most likely failure: Angular transmittance of the diagonal fins (Option B) — if the oblique geometry cannot be modeled accurately, compare A vs. C only
  • Backup route: If coupled simulation is too slow, run daylight-only (Radiance) and use a simplified cooling estimate from shading coefficients
  • What can be simplified: Report illuminance at one desk position instead of a full spatial grid

Branch Statement

I will use Route A (Retrospective Redesign) to examine how Freewan’s comparison of three fixed shading devices reveals a cooling-vs-daylight trade-off that the design team cannot resolve by instinct, through a controlled comparison of three facade options tested under identical conditions on the same office geometry, because the option that wins on energy loses on daylight and the minimum useful output is a comparison table showing cooling-load reduction and desk-level illuminance for each device against pre-stated thresholds.

Why This Example Works as an A2

  1. The decision is spatial and architectural. It starts from a facade, a room, an orientation — not a dataset. The simulation serves the design question.

  2. The ranking is not obvious. Vertical fins preserve the most daylight but deliver the weakest cooling relief. Egg-crate saves the most energy but drops illuminance below the task threshold. Diagonal fins fall between on energy but produce the darkest mornings. No single winner exists — the evidence reveals a trade-off the design team must negotiate.

  3. The controlled variable is clear. Only the shading device changes. Room geometry, glazing, orientation, climate file, and occupancy are held constant across all four conditions (three devices plus base case).

  4. Thresholds are stated before the simulation runs. Cooling reduction ≥ 10%; illuminance ≥ 300 lux. This prevents the post-hoc narrative of “well, 200 lux is close enough.”

  5. The weakest link is named. The diagonal fin’s angular transmittance is flagged as the most fragile input, with a stated backup plan (drop Option B and compare A vs. C only).

  6. The tool choice follows the question. Coupled thermal-daylight simulation is chosen because the question requires both cooling-load and illuminance data on the same geometry — not because it is the most advanced option. A spreadsheet approach is explicitly rejected because it cannot capture how shading geometry interacts with seasonal solar angles.

  7. It is grounded in a real publication. Every number in the comparison table comes from Freewan’s peer-reviewed study. Students can read the full paper to see how the analysis was done and verify the findings independently.

TipAdapting This to Your Studio Project

You do not need a shading study. Replace the three devices with any set of design alternatives you are comparing:

  • Three site layouts → GIS or Grasshopper, evaluated by walkability, noise exposure, or solar access
  • Three structural systems → BIM/BEM, evaluated by embodied carbon, span, or construction sequence
  • Three ventilation strategies → EnergyPlus or CFD, evaluated by air change rate, energy use, or thermal comfort

Keep the structure: one controlled variable, same evaluation method, stated thresholds, named weakest input, and a comparison table where the ranking might change depending on which metric leads. That tension is what makes the evidence useful.

1. **The decision is spatial and architectural.** It starts from a facade, a room, an orientation — not a dataset. The simulation serves the design question.

2. **The ranking is not obvious.** Vertical fins preserve the most daylight but deliver the weakest cooling relief. Egg-crate saves the most energy but drops illuminance below the task threshold. Diagonal fins fall between on energy but produce the darkest mornings. No single winner exists — the evidence reveals a trade-off the design team must negotiate.

3. **The controlled variable is clear.** Only the shading device changes. Room geometry, glazing, orientation, climate file, and occupancy are held constant across all four conditions (three devices plus base case).

4. **Thresholds are stated before the simulation runs.** Cooling reduction ≥ 10%; illuminance ≥ 300 lux. This prevents the post-hoc narrative of "well, 200 lux is close enough."

5. **The weakest link is named.** The diagonal fin's angular transmittance is flagged as the most fragile input, with a stated backup plan (drop Option B and compare A vs. C only).

6. **The tool choice follows the question.** Coupled thermal-daylight simulation is chosen because the question requires both cooling-load and illuminance data on the same geometry — not because it is the most advanced option. A spreadsheet approach is explicitly rejected because it cannot capture how shading geometry interacts with seasonal solar angles.

7. **It is grounded in a real publication.** Every number in the comparison table comes from Freewan's peer-reviewed study. Students can read the full paper to see how the analysis was done and verify the findings independently.

::: {.callout-tip}
## Adapting This to Your Studio Project

You do not need a shading study. Replace the three devices with any set of design alternatives you are comparing:

- **Three site layouts** → GIS or Grasshopper, evaluated by walkability, noise exposure, or solar access
- **Three structural systems** → BIM/BEM, evaluated by embodied carbon, span, or construction sequence
- **Three ventilation strategies** → EnergyPlus or CFD, evaluated by air change rate, energy use, or thermal comfort

Keep the structure: one controlled variable, same evaluation method, stated thresholds, named weakest input, and a comparison table where the ranking might change depending on which metric leads. That tension is what makes the evidence useful.
:::
